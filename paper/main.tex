\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We study how the May 2022 increase in Peru's national minimum wage, from 930 to
1,025 soles, affected workers with different levels of education. Because Peru sets
a single national minimum wage, the reform is common to all workers in time;
identification instead exploits differential exposure by skill. Low-education
workers (secondary schooling or less) cluster around the minimum wage and are
therefore bound by it, whereas high-education workers are far less likely to be bound and
provide a lower-exposure comparison group. Using the quarterly ENAHO
surveys for 2021--2024 and a difference-in-differences design with a dynamic
event-study specification, we find that the reform did not raise the real hourly
wages of low-education workers relative to high-education workers. It is instead
associated with a decline in the probability of holding formal, salaried employment
and with a shift toward self-employment, together with a modest reduction in the
employment rate. Wage effects are precisely estimated nulls and pre-trends are flat;
the employment and formality margins are less robust and we bound them using the
sensitivity analysis of \citet{rambachan_roth_2023}. The pattern is consistent with
a minimum wage that, in a labor market with pervasive informality and weak
enforcement, reallocates low-skilled workers across employment arrangements rather
than lifting their pay. We replicate the design out of sample using the January 2025
reform. The findings qualify the ``lighthouse'' view of the Peruvian minimum wage
and highlight the informal margin as the central channel of adjustment.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} minimum wage; informality; education; difference-in-differences; Peru.\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} J23, J31, J38, J46, O17.
\end{abstract}
