\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Mechanisms}

The picture that emerges is of a minimum wage that, in the Peruvian setting, has little
purchase on the pay of the workers it is meant to protect. The wage null is my most robust
result, and it holds across two reforms, across the wage distribution, and across
demographic groups. Two features of the institutional environment make this intelligible.
The first is inflation. The 2022 increase of ten percent in nominal terms coincided with
consumer price inflation of roughly eight percent, so the real value of the floor barely
moved and then eroded. The second, and more fundamental, is informality. The overwhelming
majority of low-skilled Peruvian workers are informal or self-employed, and for them the
statutory minimum is not enforced. Compliance is high only within registered formal firms,
which employ a minority of the low-skilled. A wage floor that reaches only a small,
selected part of the relevant labor force cannot be expected to move the average wage of
that force by much, and the earnings distribution in Figure~\ref{fig:dist} confirms that it
did not.

If the reform did not raise wages, the natural question is whether it had any effect at all.
Around the 2022 reform I find evidence of a shift from formal employment toward
self-employment, concentrated among the young and among women. The most plausible mechanism
is the classic one of a binding floor in the covered sector inducing some reallocation to
the uncovered sector, the displacement channel emphasized by \citet{jaramillo_lopez_2006}
for Peru and by \citet{ham_2018_honduras} for Honduras. The rise in hours in the main occupation is
consistent with movement toward self-employment, where hours are less constrained. I am
cautious, however, because this compositional effect does not replicate around the 2025
reform. The reversal suggests that at least part of what I observe around 2022 reflects the
turbulent, uneven recovery of the post-pandemic labor market rather than a structural
response to the minimum wage. I therefore present the reallocation as a documented feature
of the 2022 episode rather than as a general law.

\subsection{Reconciliation with the previous literature}

My findings both agree and disagree with the prior evidence, and the pattern of agreement
is informative. They agree with the older Peruvian work of \citet{jaramillo_lopez_2006},
who concluded that the 2003 reform was largely innocuous and found displacement toward
informality, and with \citet{cespedes_2005}, who found effects concentrated among the young.
They agree with the broad international consensus that minimum wage increases in the range
studied produce small employment effects \citep{card_krueger_1995, cengiz_2019}.

They disagree, however, with the most recent Peruvian study. \citet{jimenez_2023} finds that
the 2016 reform raised formal wages by four to five percent and increased formality, a
lighthouse pattern that I do not reproduce for 2022. Several differences plausibly account
for the divergence. The 2016 reform occurred in a low-inflation period, so its real bite was
larger and more durable than that of the inflation-eroded 2022 increase. The identification
differs: \citet{jimenez_2023} uses a department-level dose defined on the formal wage
distribution and estimates effects for the department, whereas I contrast low- and
high-education workers at the individual level, a design that places more weight on the
large informal segment of the low-skilled. And the periods differ fundamentally: 2016 was a
period of stable growth, while 2022 was a post-pandemic recovery. The contrast suggests that
the labor-market effects of the Peruvian minimum wage are state-dependent, larger and more
lighthouse-like when the real increase is substantial and the economy is stable, and muted
when inflation erodes the increase and informality absorbs the adjustment.

\subsection{External validity and limitations}

Several limitations bound the interpretation of my estimates. First, identification rests
on the assumption that, absent the reform, low- and high-education workers would have
followed parallel paths. I have tested this where possible and bounded the consequences of
its failure, but I cannot rule out differential shocks correlated with education,
particularly in the volatile 2021--2022 window. This is why I lean on the wage result,
whose pre-trends are flat and which replicates out of sample, and treat the employment-level
result as inconclusive. Second, the design identifies effects on low-skilled workers relative
to high-skilled workers. If the reform also affected high-skilled workers, for example
through general-equilibrium spillovers or ripple effects up the wage distribution, my
estimates understate or overstate the absolute effect on the low-skilled. I view large
spillovers to the tertiary-educated as unlikely given that their median wage sits well above
the floor, but I cannot exclude them. Because the comparison group is not entirely unexposed,
any bias from imperfect exposure attenuates my estimates toward zero, so the wage null is if
anything a conservative reading. Third, the data are repeated cross-sections, so I cannot
follow individuals across the reform and cannot separate changes in the composition of the
employed from changes in individual outcomes. Fourth, informality for 2024 is measured with a
reconstructed indicator rather than the official one; my robustness checks suggest this does
not drive the results, but it introduces measurement error. Finally, my window covers at most
eleven post-reform quarters, so I speak to short- and medium-run effects and cannot address
the long-run adjustment of capital or firm entry and exit.

\subsection{Policy implications}

The results carry a sober message for minimum wage policy in a high-informality economy. A
statutory floor that is not enforced for the majority of its intended beneficiaries does
little to raise their pay, and to the extent it binds in the formal sector it may push some
workers toward less protected arrangements. Raising the minimum wage is therefore an
imperfect instrument for improving the earnings of the low-skilled in Peru, a conclusion
that echoes \citet{jaramillo_lopez_2006}. This does not imply that the minimum wage is
harmless or useless; my estimates are relative and my window is short. But it does suggest
that complementary policies aimed at formalization and enforcement are prerequisites for the
minimum wage to reach the workers it is designed to protect. Absent such policies, the main
margin of adjustment is the composition of employment rather than the level of pay.
